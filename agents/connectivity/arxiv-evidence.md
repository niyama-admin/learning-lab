## B. Recommended learning path (8 papers, reading order)

1. 2505.02279 (survey of MCP/ACP/A2A/ANP): get the vocabulary and the division of labour (MCP = tools, A2A = agents) before any threat model.
2. 2503.23278 (Hou, MCP landscape and threat taxonomy): lifecycle-based threat model gives the frame that every later attack and defence paper plugs into. Swap or add 2603.18063 (MCP-38) if you want an OWASP-mapped checklist.
3. 2605.22333 (authentication measurement of remote MCP servers): turns the taxonomy into real-world numbers (40.55% without auth, 96.6% DCR flaws) and shows why the runtime cannot trust server-side auth. Pair with 2607.11086 for scanner reliability.
4. 2609.10871 (A2ABreak): applies the same adversarial lens to A2A and surfaces the identity-loss-in-delegation problem that motivates the next paper.
5. 2501.09674 (Authenticated Delegation): the design answer to "whom is the agent acting for and with what scope"; read 2603.24775 (AIP) next if you want concrete token chains.
6. 2503.18813 (CaMeL): the cleanest example of enforcing policy on tool calls with provenance outside the model; follow with 2504.11703 (Progent) for the least-privilege policy variant.
7. 2602.09433 (AARM): puts enforcement into a deployable architecture (gateway vs SDK vs eBPF) with audit receipts; MCP-Guard (2508.10991) is the empirical contrast.
8. 2406.13352 (AgentDojo): last, so you can judge every earlier defence claim against the benchmark it was measured on; then read 2510.05244 for why those benchmarks saturate.

## C. What arXiv shows about gateways and authorization products

Findings:
- Gateway and guard-proxy designs exist as research prototypes: MCP Guardian 2504.12757 (WAF-style middleware, small team, abstract gives no numbers), MCP-Guard 2508.10991 (96.01% accuracy on the authors' own GPT-4-augmented benchmark), tool-boundary firewalls 2510.05244, and the AARM specification 2602.09433 (spec only, no measurements). Policy engines are in Progent 2504.11703, SEAgent 2601.11893, CaMeL 2503.18813, Agenra 2608.22868, ARM 2604.04035.
- Audit: 2604.05485 reports 8.3 ms median overhead for pre-execution mediation with tamper-evident records, and 617 security findings across six open-source projects (abstract). 2512.17259 is proposal-only.
- Scanners are weak: 2607.11086 (<50% true positives among sampled alerts, abstract) and 2509.24272 (scanners "insufficient"). A gateway that relies on a scanner inherits this.
- Humans: only one permission-behaviour study found (2610.06047, 18 interviews + 115 survey responses, qualitative). No arXiv paper found that measures approval fatigue with a controlled experiment; web results cite figures (for example 13.6% detection of planted dangerous commands, 93% or 97% approval rates) from blogs that I did NOT trace to an arXiv paper and could not verify. Do not cite them.
- Identity/authorization: standards-body view in 2510.25819 (OpenID Foundation) and gap analysis in 2604.23280; implementations in 2501.09674 and 2603.24775. IETF drafts on OAuth on-behalf-of for agents exist (web search only; expired -02 draft seen in snippets); not on arXiv and not verified by me.

Conflicts of interest and quality flags:
- Authors evaluating their own defence on their own benchmark: 2508.10991, 2603.24775 (600 self-run attacks, 100% rejection), 2604.05969 (91% "theoretical coverage"), 2510.05244 (says benchmarks are weak while reporting perfect scores on them).
- Single-author preprints: 2602.09433 (AARM), 2603.24775 (AIP), 2604.04035, 2512.17259, 2602.09433. Two-author: 2604.05969. Small teams without evaluation numbers: 2504.12757, 2603.18063.
- Standards-body authored: 2510.25819 (OpenID Foundation whitepaper, 21 authors) is consensus guidance, and may favour OAuth/OIDC-based solutions.
- CaMeL 2503.18813 and the AARM spec have industry-linked code or governance per secondary sources (google-research repo; CSA); the abs pages themselves do not say, so treat as unverified. Affiliations in general were not verified because abs pages omit them.
- Vendor-authored papers: I cannot confirm any as vendor-authored from the abs pages alone. 2504.16902 (MAESTRO-based A2A guidance) reads as practitioner-style; its authors' employers were not verified.

Gaps (what arXiv does NOT show):
- No independent evaluation of any commercial MCP or agent gateway product (searches for gateway/guard returned only vendor pages and blogs; the only ACL-venue paper surfaced, ShieldMCP, is in the ACL Anthology, not arXiv, and was not checked).
- No arXiv study of a production A2A deployment; A2ABreak is a spec-level analysis, 2511.03841 evaluates A2A only from literature.
- No head-to-head comparison of gateway architectures (proxy vs SDK vs eBPF) with a shared benchmark; AARM names the options but does not measure them.
- No controlled human-in-the-loop approval-fatigue experiment on arXiv found; only the qualitative 2610.06047.
- MCP benchmarks (MCPTox, MSB, MCPSecBench, MCP-SafetyBench) test models and hosts, not gateways; defence-side results come from different benchmarks, so numbers are not comparable across papers.
- Benchmark saturation (2510.05244) means "0% attack success" claims are weak evidence.

Plain-language conclusion for an engineer: the threats are well characterised and measured (about 40% of remote MCP servers expose tools with no auth; tool poisoning succeeds broadly), and the credible defences are deterministic checks at the tool-call boundary (capabilities and provenance as in CaMeL, least-privilege policies as in Progent, delegation tokens as in 2501.09674 and AIP) rather than model-based filters or scanners. Gateway designs on arXiv are prototypes or specs; treat any product claim as unverified by independent research. Build the enforcement point yourself or pilot one against AgentDojo and MCPTox-style tests before trusting it.
